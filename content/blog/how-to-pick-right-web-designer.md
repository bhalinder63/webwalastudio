Anyone can call themselves a "web designer" — the range runs from a student with a Canva template to a full studio with a proper process. Since the title tells you nothing on its own, here's what to actually check, and the specific questions that separate the two ends of that range.

## The criteria that actually predict a good outcome

**Relevant portfolio, not just a pretty one.** A designer's best work might be a striking one-off for a brand very unlike yours. What matters more is whether they've built for businesses similar to yours in size and industry — a school, a clinic, a retailer — since that shows they understand the content and functionality your type of business actually needs, not just visual polish.

**A defined process, not "send us your info."** Good designers can describe their process in specifics: what happens after you pay, how many revision rounds are included, and roughly how long each stage takes. If the answer is vague or entirely open-ended, that's usually because there isn't a real process behind it.

**Mobile-first execution, not mobile-friendly claims.** Almost everyone claims "responsive design" now. The only way to actually verify it is to open their past work on your own phone and check that it loads fast, that text doesn't need zooming, and that buttons are easy to tap — not just that the layout doesn't break.

**Clear ownership terms.** This is the criterion most people skip and regret later. Ask plainly: after launch, do you own the code, the domain, and the hosting account outright, or does the designer retain control? Designers who build on locked, proprietary platforms — or who keep the only login — turn a simple future change into a negotiation.

**Transparent, itemized pricing.** A trustworthy quote separates the build cost from ongoing costs like domain and hosting, and states what's included (page count, revision rounds) rather than a single vague number. If a price seems unusually low next to comparable quotes, the gap is almost always paid for later, in scope or support.

**Real post-launch support.** A website needs small fixes and updates after it goes live — that's normal, not a sign something went wrong. Ask what's included after launch, for how long, and what support costs once that period ends. A designer who goes quiet after the final invoice is one of the most common complaints in this industry.

## Freelancer, agency, or DIY builder?

- **DIY builders** (Wix, Squarespace) are the cheapest option and fine for a hobby page, but you're doing the design work yourself, and the result usually looks and performs like it.
- **Freelancers** can be excellent and affordable, but quality varies more, and there's no backup if they become unavailable mid-project.
- **A small studio or agency** typically costs more than a freelancer but less than a large agency, and gives you a defined process and more reliability than a single individual working alone.

None of these is universally "right" — the criteria above matter more than which category a designer falls into.

## Frequently asked questions

**How can I tell if a web designer's portfolio is trustworthy?**
Ask for live URLs, not screenshots — screenshots can be cropped or borrowed. Open the real sites on your own phone, and check that they still look current; a portfolio full of sites from years ago (or ones that no longer load) is a sign the "recent work" isn't actually recent.

**Is a lower price always a red flag?**
Not always, but it's worth asking why the price is lower — is it a genuinely leaner process, or is it missing things like mobile testing, revisions, or post-launch support that a higher quote includes? Compare price against what's included, not the number alone.

**Should I choose a designer who specializes in my industry?**
It helps, but it's not required. What matters more is whether they can show they understand the *functionality* your industry needs — a booking flow for a salon, an inventory-aware catalog for a retailer — even if their past clients were in a different industry.

**How many years of experience should a web designer have?**
Years matter less than a verifiable track record of finished, live, working sites. A newer studio with five strong live examples is generally a safer bet than a designer with a decade of experience and no live work you can actually check.

**Do client reviews and testimonials actually matter?**
Yes, but read them for specifics, not star ratings alone. A review that mentions the timeline held, communication was responsive, or a bug was fixed quickly after launch tells you far more than a generic five-star comment.

**What's the single question most people forget to ask?**
"What happens if I want to make a change six months after launch?" It surfaces the ownership and support answers in one shot, and a vague response there is usually the clearest warning sign available before you've paid anything.
