"Maintenance included" is one of the most under-explained phrases in web design — most business owners agree to it without knowing what it actually covers, then are surprised later by what it doesn't. Here's a straight breakdown.

## What genuinely needs attention after a site goes live

**Bug fixes.** No matter how thoroughly a site is tested before launch, real-world usage across different devices and browsers surfaces small issues — a button that doesn't align on one phone model, a form that fails on a specific browser. This is completely normal and exactly what a maintenance period should cover.

**Small content changes.** Updated pricing, a new team member's bio, a changed phone number — these are minor edits that shouldn't require a new invoice every time, at least during an initial maintenance window.

**Security and platform updates.** The software underlying most websites needs periodic updates to stay secure. Skipped long enough, this is how sites get hacked or start breaking unexpectedly.

**Renewals.** Domain and hosting are annual costs that need renewing on schedule — miss one and your entire site can go offline without warning, which is a surprisingly common and entirely avoidable failure.

## What maintenance typically does *not* cover

- A full redesign or new pages beyond what was originally agreed.
- New functionality that wasn't part of the original build (adding e-commerce to a site that didn't have it, for example).
- Content you haven't provided yet — maintenance keeps existing content current, it doesn't create new content for you.

Knowing this distinction upfront avoids an awkward conversation later about what should have been a new project versus what should have been covered.
